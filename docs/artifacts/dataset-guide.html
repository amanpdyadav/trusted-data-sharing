The big picture

All ten datasets describe one fake population of 100,000 people in Southwest Finland. The same person can be a hospital patient, a bus rider, a bank customer and a telecom caller, so data can be linked across datasets, just like in real life. Real postcodes and town names are used for geography only; every person, organisation and number is generated.

Health in detail

ed_visits has 29 columns: one row per emergency-department visit, with identity, arrival, triage, diagnoses, vital signs, physician and outcome. It joins to lab_results and clinical_notes by visit_id. Notes are in Finnish, Swedish or English.

Hidden identifiers

The personal ID code (HETU) encodes birth date and sex. Ambulance unit codes reveal the patient's area even when the postcode is gone. Sex-specific lab reference ranges reveal sex. Lab sample times re-link a de-keyed lab table to visits.

Rare, near-unique values

Helicopter arrivals, rare chronic diseases (cystic fibrosis, ALS, haemophilia) and rural postcodes with few residents can single out one person.

Deliberate data-quality traps

Temperatures recorded in °F, heart-rate glitches (0, 250, 300), swapped blood-pressure values, discharge before arrival, and duplicate registrations. Cleaning these is a small bonus win.

Staff are data subjects too

Physician names and IDs, and author names on notes, need protecting. Shift patterns can identify staff.

The key design insight: attackers match on the exact arrival date and time, but the utility tasks need only the hour of day and dates precise enough to count 30-day revisits. So we can split the timestamp: keep the arrival hour as its own column, shift each patient's dates by a secret per-patient offset (which keeps the gaps between visits), and report coarse dates. That breaks the news and social-post matching while keeping H-U6 and H-U8 intact. This is the kind of scenario-aware choice that beats the k ≥ 5 baseline, whose utility drops to 0.40.

Customer service in detail

What it is

140,345 utterances from chats, phone transcripts (speech-recognition output, lowercase, no punctuation) and emails, in Finnish (73%), English (20%) and Swedish (6%).

Labelled for us

8,981 conversations come with labelled personal-data spans we can test against. 2,962 more form a hidden evaluation set the organisers score.

Hardest cases

ID codes and phone numbers read out as words (“nolla neljä nolla”), spelled-out emails, passwords typed into chat, first names on their own, and numbers split across turns.

Most common labels

Person names 38,401 · account numbers 11,600 · national ID codes 10,031 · phones 6,143 · dates 3,995 · addresses 3,173 · emails 2,698.

Regex alone fails on spoken and multilingual text, which is why the benchmark calls free text the place where most teams can win. An LLM that understands Finnish and Swedish, checked by rules for formats like IBANs and ID codes, is our main edge here. Scoring rewards recall (F2), so missing an identifier costs more than over-redacting.

Unseen dataset

An HR and payroll export from a fictional care company: 2,818 rows and 26 columns named only c01 to c26, with mixed formats and no labels. Our detector has to work out what each column is from the values alone. The organisers hold the answers and can score our labels, which is a fair test that we can't overfit.

What this changes in the plan

  • Transform: split arrival time into hour-of-day plus per-patient shifted dates; keep one consistent keyed pseudonym across visits, labs and notes; derive age band from the ID code, then drop the code.
  • Detect: look beyond column names. Values like ambulance codes, lab reference ranges and sample times leak identity, and the field catalog's “traps” column lists them for checking our detector.
  • Free text: design for three languages and speech-recognition text from day one.
  • Data quality: fix the deliberate glitches (°F, swapped blood pressure, impossible heart rates) and mention it in the trust report.
